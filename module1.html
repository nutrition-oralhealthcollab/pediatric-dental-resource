Module 01 • Editorial Intelligence

Foundations of Pediatric Oral Health

neurology

Learning Objectives:

The primary dentition is not merely a precursor to permanent teeth; it serves as a critical structural anchor for the oral phase of swallowing. Feeding specialists (SLPs/OTs) must recognize that tooth eruption patterns directly influence bolus management and lingual coordination.

Section 1: The Dental Arches and Occlusion

The maxillary (upper) and mandibular (lower) dental arches house the teeth and define the child's bite relationship (occlusion). Occlusion matters to feeding teams because it determines how effectively a child can break down food.

• Overjet: Horizontal distance between upper and lower incisors. Significant overjet is common in children with prolonged pacifier use or digit sucking and can also be a risk factor for dental trauma.
• Open bite: A gap between upper and lower teeth when the back teeth are together. Anterior open bites can arise from pacifier or digit use.
• Posterior crossbite: Upper back teeth occlude inside the lower back teeth.

Observing the oral cavity during a feeding assessment is within every provider's scope and recognizing abnormalities and communicating them to dental colleagues is a core interprofessional skill.

Classification Description
Class I (Normal) Correct anteroposterior relationship; maxillary incisors overlap mandibular incisors by ~2 mm vertically and horizontally.
Class II, Division 1 Retruded mandible with proclined upper incisors (increased overjet).
Class II, Division 2 Retruded mandible with retroclined upper incisors (deep bite).
Class III Protruded mandible relative to maxilla (underbite).

Section 2: Dentition

Humans traditionally have two sets of dentition: primary (baby teeth) and permanent (adult teeth). While there are estimated ranges for eruption and exfoliation of teeth, they vary on the individual and sometimes within families. analytics View Tooth Growth Chart

Primary (deciduous) dentition: 20 teeth total. Eruption begins around 6 months of age and is complete by approximately 30 months of age. Primary teeth are smaller, whiter, and have proportionally larger pulp (nerve) chambers than permanent teeth.

Permanent (adult) dentition: 32 teeth (including third molars (wisdom teeth)). Transition begins around age 6 and completes in the late teens to early twenties.

Mixed Dentition Period

Ages ~6–12, during which primary and permanent teeth coexist. This is a period of significant oral change and vulnerability to malocclusion if primary teeth are lost prematurely or if a patient is experiencing a persistent oral habit, such as thumb-sucking.

Primary teeth are essential for chewing, speech, and holding space for permanent successors. They are necessary for normal and symmetrical growth and development. While the front teeth are typically lost early in the tooth exfoliation sequence, the early loss of primary molars can cause space loss, impacted teeth, and biting discrepancies.

Early tooth loss from caries (cavities) or trauma can compromise chewing function, nutrition, and arch development which is all directly relevant to feeding teams.

Tissue Composition
  • • Enamel: outermost mineralized crown covering
  • • Dentin: bulk of tooth structure
  • • Cementum: anchors the root surface
Periodontium

Includes periodontal ligament, alveolar bone, and gingiva, providing sensory feedback for bite regulation.

Section 3: Dental Anomalies and Feeding Intersections

According to the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry, children should have an established dental home no later than 12 months of age. Doing so fosters regular care habits from the start and creates opportunities for anticipatory guidance, preventive interventions, and both acute and comprehensive oral health services, making early detection and prevention far more achievable.

With a dental home established early, many of these concerns can be identified and addressed proactively. However, as a member of the feeding team, you may also encounter dental anomalies in the course of your own observations. These findings can certainly warrant a dental referral, particularly when a dental home has not yet been established.

Anomalies of Tooth Number

One category of dental anomalies involves the number of teeth present, which can range from fewer teeth than expected to more teeth than expected.

Hypodontia, Oligodontia, and Anodontia: Hypodontia, defined as the congenital absence of one to five teeth excluding third molars, is among the most common dental anomalies. Oligodontia (6+ missing teeth) and anodontia (complete absence) are rarer but carry greater functional and long-term consequences. Children with hypodontia often experience increased difficulty chewing once primary teeth associated with missing permanent teeth are lost.

Supernumerary Teeth: Extra teeth beyond the typical number. These can lead to crowding, impaction, or malocclusion and may be associated with syndromes including Cleidocranial Dysplasia, Gardner Syndrome, or Cleft Palate.

Anomalies of Tooth Structure

A second category involves the structure of the teeth themselves. When enamel forms abnormally, functional and comfort-related consequences for eating can be significant.

Amelogenesis Imperfecta (AI): A group of hereditary disorders affecting enamel formation. Enamel may be thin, soft, or brittle. Rapid wear reduces functional occlusal surfaces, while severe sensitivity causes pain during eating. Children frequently develop compensatory behaviors, avoiding hard, cold, or hot foods.

Enamel Hypoplasia and Molar-Incisor Hypomineralization (MIH): Affects at least one of the four first permanent molars. These teeth are more susceptible to decay, sensitivity, and structural failure, reducing functional dentition available for chewing.
