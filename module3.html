Module 3: Diet & Oral Health Risk

Learning Objectives:

  1. Explain why some foods and drinks cause more cavities. Cover how easily a food turns into sugar bacteria can use, how often it's eaten, and how long it stays on teeth. Apply this to common pediatric feeding foods/drinks.
  2. Spot risky feeding habits and help families reduce cavity risk without disrupting feeding therapy. This includes nighttime feeding, prolonged bottle use, frequent sipping, and long meals. Give practical advice that protects teeth while still supporting feeding therapy goals.
  3. Explain fluoride's role in preventing cavities and guide families on safe use by age. Cover fluoride sources, age-appropriate amounts, and how to use it safely and effectively.

Section 1: Cariogenic Food & Beverages

Many of the children we share, particularly those with dysphagia, developmental disabilities, and oral motor difficulties, are at elevated caries risk due to factors inherent to their conditions and their dietary management. Our goal is to support collaborative decision-making that optimizes both safe feeding and oral health. 

science How it Happens: The Demineralization Process

1. Acid Production

Bacteria metabolize sugars/carbs, producing acid as a byproduct within 3-5 minutes.

2. pH Drop

Plaque pH drops below the critical threshold of 5.5, causing enamel to lose calcium and phosphate ions.

3. Biofilm Formation

Sucrose synthesizes into extracellular glucans, enhancing bacterial adhesion and plaque formation.

High Cariogenic Potential Categories

water_drop Liquid Sugars

Sugar-sweetened beverages, fruit juices, and flavored milks. Impact is compounded when offered in bottles or sippy cups between meals.

layers Sticky & Retentive

Dried fruits, fruit snacks, gummy vitamins, and chewy candies. These adhere to surfaces and prolong acid exposure.

bakery_dining Processed Starches

Crackers, cookies, and sweetened cereals. Cariogenic potential increases significantly with processing.

prescriptions Sugared Medications

Flavored liquid syrups. A frequently overlooked source, especially for children with chronic conditions.

Low-Risk & Protective Food Options

Cariostatic Foods (Cheese)

Not all foods pose the same risk to dental health, and an understanding of lower-risk and protective options can help make more informed dietary recommendations. Cheese is considered cariostatic, meaning it has the ability to inhibit or prevent the formation and progression of dental caries, largely due to its calcium and phosphate content.

Mechanical Cleansing (Fibrous Vegetables)

Raw vegetables such as carrots, celery, and broccoli offer a dual benefit, as their fibrous texture stimulates salivary flow while also providing a degree of mechanical cleansing of tooth surfaces.

Natural vs. Added Sugars (Fruits & Dairy)

Fresh whole fruits contain natural sugars within a fiber matrix that slows sugar release. Unsweetened dairy products are also a lower-risk choice, as lactose is less cariogenic than sucrose and milk proteins offer additional protective effects.

Hydration (Fluoridated Water)

Plain water, and particularly fluoridated tap water, remains the ideal between-meal beverage and is the recommended choice for daytime sipping.

Section 2: Feeding Patterns & Oral Health

Clinical Considerations

Children receiving care from feeding teams often have extra risk factors for early childhood cavities. Many children with oral motor challenges stay on bottles or sippy cups well past the recommended weaning age of 12 months.

When used for formula, milk, or juice, liquid pools around teeth for long periods. Nocturnal feeding is especially risky; if a night feed is necessary, offer water afterward or gently wipe gums and teeth with a damp cloth to mitigate risk.

home Important Milestone: Dental Home

The dental home should be established no later than 12 months of age to help children and their families institute a lifetime of optimal oral health.

Key Takeaway: Caregiver Role

Children lack the fine motor dexterity to brush effectively until age 7-8. Until this milestone, a parent or caregiver must perform the brushing to ensure adequate plaque removal.

Preventive Hygiene Protocols

  • Brushing Frequency: Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste. Brushing after the last feed of the day is the most critical, as saliva flow decreases overnight.
  • Toothpaste Amounts: Use a rice-grain-sized amount for children under 3, and a pea-sized amount for ages 3 to 6.
  • Alternatives: If a child cannot tolerate brushing, wiping teeth and gums with a damp cloth after feeds is an effective alternative.

Nutritional & Behavioral Strategies

  • Food Choices: Prioritize lower-cavity-risk options like cheese, plain yogurt, avocado, eggs, and nut butters over sweetened purees or sticky snacks.
  • Meal Structure: Group calorie intake into set meal times rather than grazing all day. Offer plain water during long meals to rinse sugar and acid off the teeth.
  • Professional Care: For frequent feeders, request fluoride varnish every 3 months (instead of the usual 6) to offset higher risk.

clinical_notes Practical Clinical Approaches for Feeding Teams

  • Strategic Hydration: Offer plain water periodically during prolonged meals to rinse sugar or acid content from tooth surfaces between bites/sips.
  • Meal Structure: Consolidate caloric intake into defined meal periods rather than grazing throughout the day.
  • Dental Coordination: For children requiring frequent feeds, coordinate with the dental team for increased fluoride exposure (e.g., varnish every 3 months).
  • Safe Sleep Habits: Avoid putting a child to bed with a bottle containing anything other than water.
  • Critical Hygiene: Clean teeth after the last feeding of the day. This is the most important brushing as salivary flow is lowest overnight, reducing natural acid neutralization.

Section 3: Fluoride Sources & Safe Use

Fluoride protects teeth through three primary mechanisms: incorporating into the enamel crystal structure to form acid-resistant fluorapatite, promoting remineralization of early enamel damage (such as white-spot lesions), and inhibiting bacterial metabolism in dental plaque.
