Module 5: Special Populations + Interprofessional Care Planning

Learning Objectives

  1. Understand dental risks tied to common diagnoses (Premature birth, Down syndrome, CP, Autism, CHD).
  2. Recognize how medications affect the mouth (dry mouth, gingival hyperplasia, enamel issues, yeast).
  3. Understand acid reflux impact and the necessity for collaborative management strategies.
  4. Connect children with dental care by effectively flagging concerns and managing follow-up.
  5. Utilize a shared framework to integrate dental health goals into comprehensive care plans.

Section 1: Special Populations

Premature Birth

High incidence of enamel hypoplasia. Early intubation may impact palate shape and primary tooth eruption patterns.

Down Syndrome

Hypodontia (missing teeth), delayed eruption, and significant risk for early-onset periodontal disease and macroglossia issues.

Cerebral Palsy

Increased risk of bruxism, trauma due to falls, and oral hygiene challenges linked to spasticity and hyperactive gag reflex.

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Sensory aversions making brushing difficult. Preference for soft, sticky carbohydrates often leads to high caries risk.

Congenital Heart Disease

Critical need for excellent oral health to prevent infective endocarditis. Frequent coordination with cardiology for procedures.

Premature Birth

When premature babies need a breathing tube through the mouth for a long time, the pressure can reshape the roof of the mouth, leaving it high, narrow, and grooved. This happens in up to 48% of the tiniest babies (those born under about 2.2 pounds), and it can make learning to feed by mouth harder. Some may need their milk or formula thickened to help them swallow safely. The effects can last for years. By age 5, children born prematurely are much more likely than their full-term peers to have a narrow, poorly formed palate (about 41%), teeth that don't line up properly (about 56%), and weak tooth enamel (about 22%). Being on a breathing machine at birth directly raises the risk of enamel problems.

Down Syndrome

Delayed dental eruption and hypodontia occur in ~23% of children with Down syndrome, which can affect chewing development and texture progression. In Down syndrome, periodontal disease and feeding difficulties are linked through several overlapping pathways:

  • Pain and oral aversion. Inflamed, bleeding gums and progressive periodontitis cause oral pain that trigger food refusal, resistance to textured foods, and mealtime behavioral difficulties.
  • Tooth mobility and loss. Progressive attachment loss leads to loose teeth and eventual tooth loss, directly impairing chewing and the ability to form a food bolus.
  • Worsening malocclusion. Periodontal disease aggravates the bite problems already common in Down syndrome.
  • Salivary dysfunction. Reduced saliva flow drives periodontal disease and impairs the oral prep phase of swallowing.
  • Texture progression delays. Periodontal disease, dental anomalies (missing teeth in about 23%, delayed eruption, severe tooth wear), and oral motor dysfunction together create a compounding barrier to age-appropriate texture advancement, narrowing the range of textures a child can safely and comfortably manage.

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Children with ASD face an increased risk of cavities and gum disease due to sensory sensitivities to food (taste, feel, smell) that often lead to highly selective, sugary, or starchy diets. Dental pain frequently presents non-verbally as food refusal or a sudden narrowing of accepted textures. Management often requires behavioral strategies such as visual schedules and desensitization; in some cases, general anesthesia is necessary for comprehensive dental treatment.

Cerebral Palsy

Oral health in CP is complicated by both physical challenges and medication side effects. Medications like phenytoin can cause gingival hyperplasia (significant gum overgrowth), while muscle relaxants may suppress appetite and motility. Additionally, hyposalivation (dry mouth) is common, significantly increasing the risk of rapid tooth decay and making the management of oral hygiene a critical daily priority.

Congenital Heart Disease (CHD)

For high-risk groups, including those with cyanotic CHD or recent repairs involving prosthetic material, infective endocarditis prophylaxis (antibiotics before dental procedures) is essential. While clinical coordination is vital, daily oral hygiene remains the cornerstone of prevention, as the mouth is a primary entry point for bacteria that can affect heart valves.

Section 4: Craniofacial Conditions & Feeding Considerations

Craniofacial anomalies significantly impact the mechanics of both feeding and oral hygiene. Interdisciplinary management between the feeding team and craniofacial dental specialists is essential for optimizing long-term outcomes.

face

Cleft Lip and Palate

Focus on the challenges of achieving an intraoral seal for suction, the risk of nasal regurgitation, and the critical importance of early dental/orthodontic consultation for NAM or surgical planning.

medical_information

Micrognathia

Address airway obstruction risks during feeding, the necessity of specialized positioning (sidelying or prone), and the impact of a retruded mandible on early dental development.

clinical_notes

Hemifacial Microsomia

Detail the asymmetry of oral motor function, the potential for unilateral feeding challenges, and the long-term orthodontic implications of mandibular hypoplasia.

Craniofacial Conditions and Dental Considerations

From a dental perspective, children with cleft palate have disrupted development of the dental arch, along with teeth that may be missing or growing in abnormal positions around the cleft. These children require ongoing orthodontic management over many years, which directly affects their ability to move through food textures as their bite and dentition change over time.

A small or set-back lower jaw (micrognathia and retrognathia), seen in conditions like Pierre Robin sequence and Treacher Collins syndrome, pushes the base of the tongue backward. This narrows the airway at the back of the throat and interferes with both feeding and breathing.

From a dental perspective, the small jaw leaves too little room for teeth, leading to crowding and a poorly aligned bite (malocclusion). Some children need a surgical procedure called mandibular distraction osteogenesis, which gradually lengthens the lower jaw. This one intervention does three things at once: it opens up the airway, improves feeding ability, and creates space for teeth to develop more normally.

Underdevelopment of the upper jaw and midface (maxillary and midface hypoplasia), common in Down syndrome, Apert syndrome, and Crouzon syndrome, affects both the nasal airway and the dental arch. When the upper jaw is small or set back, the nasal airway is often narrowed, and there isn't enough room for the teeth to align properly. This contributes to crossbite (where the upper and lower teeth meet incorrectly), crowding, and less efficient chewing mechanics.

Because these problems span breathing, biting, and feeding, these children often need coordinated care across several specialties, with craniofacial surgery, orthodontics, and feeding therapy working together to align treatment as the child grows.
